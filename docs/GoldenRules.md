# Golden Rules of an Independent Game Designer

Independent game design is a craft of focus: limited time, limited budget, and unlimited ideas. These rules help you turn ideas into finished, playable games.

## 1. Build the Smallest Fun Loop First
If the core loop is not fun in a rough prototype, more features will not fix it.

## 2. Define One Clear Player Promise
State in one sentence what the player should feel and do. If a feature does not support that promise, cut it.

## 3. Scope Is a Feature
A smaller finished game beats a larger unfinished game. Choose fewer systems and polish them.

## 4. Prototype Fast, Throw Away Fast
Treat early prototypes as experiments, not investments. Keep what works, delete what does not.

## 5. Prioritize Decisions, Not Mechanics
Good design creates meaningful choices with clear trade-offs, not just more buttons to press.

## 6. Keep Rules Simple, Outcomes Deep
Simple inputs and readable systems can still produce rich strategy and replayability.

## 7. Teach Through Play
Avoid long tutorials. Use the first minutes of gameplay to teach one concept at a time.

## 8. Feedback Must Be Immediate and Clear
When players act, show results right away with visuals, audio, and UI updates.

## 9. Data-Drive Balance
Store tuning values in data files so you can iterate quickly without rewriting code.

## 10. Design for Replayability Early
Rogue-lite value comes from variation, risk, and long-term progression. Build these pillars from day one.

## 11. Limit Complexity Per Milestone
At each milestone, add one major system at most. Multiple new systems at once hide bugs and design problems.

## 12. Test With Real Players, Not Just Yourself
Your intuition is useful, but external playtests reveal confusion, friction, and broken balance.

## 13. Track What Players Actually Do
Collect simple metrics: run length, failure causes, most-picked choices, and drop-off points.

## 14. Build a Vertical Slice Early
A short, complete slice of the game proves your pipeline, your fun factor, and your production reality.

## 15. Plan Content Like a Pipeline
Create templates and naming standards for cards, events, levels, and UI. Chaos in content production kills schedules.

## 16. Keep Technical Debt Visible
Move fast, but keep a list of shortcuts and clean them at fixed intervals before they block progress.

## 17. Optimize for Iteration Speed
Fast launch times, quick reloads, and clear debug tools multiply your output more than premature optimization.

## 18. Market While You Build
Share progress regularly. Wishlists and community interest need steady momentum, not last-minute promotion.

## 19. Protect Your Energy and Time
Sustainable pace is a production strategy. Burnout is a hidden project risk.

## 20. Finish, Ship, Learn, Repeat
Shipping teaches more than polishing forever. Release, gather feedback, and apply lessons to the next version.

## Practical Checklist Before Any New Feature
- Does it strengthen the core loop?
- Can it be prototyped in less than one day?
- Can players understand it without a long explanation?
- Can it be balanced with data, not hard-coded values?
- Does it fit current scope and milestone goals?

If two or more answers are no, postpone the feature.
